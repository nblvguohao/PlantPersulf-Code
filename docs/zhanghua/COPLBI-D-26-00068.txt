Abstract:
Nitric oxide (NO) and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) have emerged as key gaseous signaling
molecules in plants, playing central roles in the regulation of responses to a myriad of
(a)biotic stresses. A major mechanism through which these molecules exert their
effects is via post-translational modifications (PTMs) of proteins, including tyrosine
nitration, S-nitrosation, and persulfidation. The last two corresponds to redox-based
modifications (oxiPTMs) that can rapidly and reversibly alter protein activity, stability,
localization, and interactions, thereby enabling dynamic cellular reprogramming under
stress conditions. Recent advances have highlighted the intricate crosstalk between
NO- and H2S-mediated PTMs, revealing a complex regulatory network that fine-tunes
stress signaling pathways and enhances plant resilience. Understanding how these
modifications integrate environmental cues with cellular and subcellular responses
provides new insights into the molecular basis of stress tolerance and offers potential
strategies for improving crop performance under adverse conditions.

Introduction 
25 
Throughout their developmental cycle, plants are exposed to a wide range of adverse abiotic 
26 
factors that can negatively affect their growth and productivity. To cope with these 
27 
challenges, they have developed complex regulatory networks involving diverse subcellular 
28 
and cellular components that orchestrate appropriate responses to minimize potential damage 
29 
[Kumar et al., 2025; Kaya and Corpas, 2026]. These responses include hormonal signaling, 
30 
fluctuations in calcium levels, activation of transcription factors, and the controlled 
31 
production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Among the molecules mobilized during these 
32 
stress responses, two gaseous compounds, nitric oxide (NO) and hydrogen sulfide (H2S), have 
33 
emerged as key regulators over the past two decades, despite having been historically 
34 
regarded as toxic [Kimura, 2026]. Both are now recognized as gasotransmitters, a class of 
35 
small signaling molecules with important roles in plant physiology and in response to stresses 
36 
[Corpas et al., 2026; Mohanty et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026]. This review discusses how 
37 
NO and H2S, through their associated post-translational modifications (PTMs), influence 
38 
plant responses to abiotic stress. 
39 
Biochemical basis of PTMs mediated by NO and H2S 
40 
NO and H2S give rise to families of derived compounds known as reactive nitrogen species 
41 
(RNS) and reactive sulfur species (RSS), respectively. These molecules exhibit a far more 
42 
complex biochemistry than might be expected from their simple structures. One of their 
43 
principal regulatory mechanisms involves interaction with specific amino acid residues, 
44 
particularly tyrosine (Tyr), tryptophan (Trp), and cysteine (Cys), in target proteins, leading to 
45 
post-translational modifications (PTMs) that modulate protein function. Among these, the 
46 
most extensively studied in higher plants are protein Tyr-nitration and S-nitrosation, mediated 
47 
by NO, and persulfidation, mediated by H2S. A key aspect of the regulatory roles of NO and 
48 
H2S is their spatial specificity, as their production, metabolism, and signaling are tightly 
49 
controlled through subcellular compartmentalization, allowing precise modulation of target 
50 
proteins and subcellular/cellular processes. 
51

52

54

55 
(•NO2) [Bartesaghi S, Radi, 2018] (Figure 1A). In higher plants, tyrosine nitration has been

58

65

66

67

68 
Vargas et al., 2023]. In the case of Trp, nitration generally occurs at the C4, C6, or C7 
69 
positions of the indole ring. Currently, increased protein nitration is widely regarded as a 
70 
marker of nitro-oxidative stress. While a specific nitrotyrosine “denitrase” activity has been 
71 
described in animal systems, there is still no experimental evidence for such an activity in 
72 
plants. Instead, protein nitration has been proposed to serve as a signal for protein degradation 
73 
[León et al., 2022].  
74 
Beyond proteins, other biomolecules are also susceptible to nitration. These include 
75 
fatty acids, such as nitro-oleic, nitro-linoleic, and nitro-linolenic acids, as well as nucleic acids 
76 
(DNA, RNA, and mRNA) or γ-tocopherol [Mata-Pérez et al., 2016; Sytykiewicz et al., 
77 
2024; Chmielowska-Bąk et al., 2025; Desel et al., 2007]. Some of these nitrated molecules 
78 
have been implicated in signaling processes and stress response, although their roles in higher 
79 
plants remain poorly understood and require further investigation.

Protein S-nitrosation 
81 
S-nitrosation, previously referred to as S-nitrosylation, involves the addition of NO to the thiol 
82 
group (-SH) of Cys residues [Gupta et al., 2020] (Figure 1B). Although proteins are the most 
83 
extensively studied targets of this modification in plant cells, other molecules, such as 
84 
peptides, including reduced glutathione (GSH) and free L-Cys, present at mM and µM 
85 
concentrations, respectively, can also undergo S-nitrosation, forming S-nitrosoglutathione 
86 
(GSNO) and nitrosocysteine (CysNO). These compounds play important roles in 
87 
transnitrosation processes and NO-mediated signaling. In this context, S-nitrosoglutathione 
88 
reductase (GSNOR), a glutathione-dependent formaldehyde dehydrogenase (FALDH), plays

Protein persulfidation 
97 
Persulfidation, previously named S-sulfhydration, is an H2S-derived PTM that involves the 
98 
conversion of a protein Cys thiol group (–SH) into its corresponding persulfide form (–SSH) 
99 
[Aroca et al., 2015; Moseler et al., 2024; Romero et al., 2025; Carrillo et al., 2026]. 
100 
Persulfidation occurs not only in proteins but also leads to the formation of other molecules 
101 
known as polysulfides. These include cysteine hydropersulfide (CysS-Sn-H), glutathione 
102 
hydropersulfide (GS-Sn-H), and glutathione trisulfide (GSSSG) [Ogata et al., 2023]. These 
103 
polysulfides are considered to be more redox-active than typical thiols and disulfides. 
104 
However, research on these molecules in plants is quite limited [Kasamatsu et al., 2023a, 
105 
2023b; Ida et al., 2026]. Persulfidation can occur through several mechanisms: (i) the 
106 
reaction between a protein thiol and H2S, particularly via its anionic hydrosulfide form (HS⁻), 
107 
which is a stronger nucleophile than the protonated species; and (ii) interactions between 
108 
inorganic polysulfides (–S–Sn–S–) and protein thiolates (Figure 1C). In addition, trans-
109 
persulfidation reactions may take place between low-molecular-weight persulfides, such as 
110 
glutathione persulfide (GSSH) and cysteine persulfide (Cys-SSH), and protein persulfides, 
111 
where GSH and L-Cys also contribute to this dynamic exchange network [Vignane and 
112 
Filipovic 2023; Nagy et al., 2026]. Importantly, persulfidation often arises from the 
113 
nucleophilic attack of a sulfide anion on oxidized forms of protein thiols, including disulfides, 
114 
mixed disulfides, cysteine sulfenic acids, or S-nitrosated cysteine residues. At the cellular 
115 
level, this modification is part of a broader regulatory framework in which oxiPTMs compete 
116 
for the same reactive Cys residues within proteins. Under conditions of oxidative stress, 
117 
modifications such as S-nitrosation and persulfidation can play a protective role by shielding 
118 
these thiol groups from irreversible overoxidation to sulfinic or sulfonic acids, which is 
119 
typically associated with loss of protein function [Corpas et al., 2022]. In this context, S-
120 
nitrosation and persulfidation act as a reversible redox switches, preserving protein activity 
121 
and contributing to the maintenance of cellular redox homeostasis.

NO- and H2S-derived PTMs in abiotic stress responses 
148 
The significance of NO- and H2S-derived PTMs lies in their ability to modulate multiple 
149 
levels of plant stress response mechanisms, including the regulation of antioxidant systems 
150 
[Zhao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2026] and an NADPH-dependent redox 
151 
homeostasis [Lin et al., 2026], as well as the feedback control of NO and H2S metabolism 
152 
themselves [Guerra et al., 2016; Muñoz-Vargas et al., 2023; Corpas et al., 2026]. Beyond 
153 
these functions, they also participate in broader regulatory networks involving transcription 
154 
factors and phytohormone signaling pathways, such as those mediated by ethylene, abscisic 
155 
acid, and cytokinins, among others [Zhang et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Wang et al., 
156 
2024; Wang et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Li et al., 
157 
2026]. Table 1 summarizes representative examples of the involvement of these PTMs in 
158 
plant responses to a wide range of abiotic stresses, including salinity, drought, hypoxia, 
159 
senescence, cadmium toxicity, freezing, alkaline conditions, and osmotic stress. Thus, the 
160 
available information support that nitration, S-nitrosation and persulfidation, play central roles 
161 
in plant adaptation to abiotic stresses. S-nitrosation regulates key processes involved in salt, 
162 
hypoxia, freezing, alkaline, drought, and cadmium stress responses by modulating proteins 
163 
associated with RNA processing, vesicle trafficking, hormone signaling, ion homeostasis, and 
164 
proline and ethylene biosynthesis. These modifications generally enhance stress tolerance by 
165 
promoting osmotic adjustment, maintaining ionic balance, and activating stress-responsive 
166 
gene expression, although excessive S-nitrosation under cadmium stress can aggravate 
167 
cellular damage. Likewise, persulfidation contributes significantly to drought and oxidative 
168 
stress tolerance by regulating ABA signaling, stomatal closure, autophagy, ROS homeostasis, 
169 
and antioxidant defenses. Key protein targets include DES1, RBOHD, SnRK2.6, ABI4, and 
170 
ATG4, whose persulfidation coordinates water conservation, redox balance, and stress 
171 
signaling. Overall, both S-nitrosation and persulfidation act as dynamic regulatory 
172 
mechanisms that integrate redox, hormonal, and metabolic pathways to enhance plant 
173 
resilience under abiotic conditions.

Conclusion and future perspectives 
187 
The endogenously generated plant gas transmitters NO and H2S have been incorporated into 
188 
protein signaling and regulation mechanisms in both physiological processes and in response 
189 
to abiotic stresses. One of the mechanisms by which NO and H2S exert their effects is through 
190 
protein PTMs, especially nitration, S-nitrosation, and persufidation. These PTMs are specific 
191 
and can either positively, negatively, or have no effect on the target protein. A particularly 
192 
relevant aspect is that in many cases, a specific protein can be simultaneously and 
193 
competitively regulated by several of these PTMs, either synergistically or antagonistically. 
194 
The final effect on its function will influence the cellular redox state, which changes 
195 
depending on the physiological status. In stress situations, there is usually an increase in ROS 
196 
and RNS. Depending on the cell's capacity to respond to a specific abiotic stress, it may 
197 
survive or undergo irreversible damage, leading to cell death. However, it is important to 
198 
realize that plants are rarely exposed to a single abiotic stress in natural environments. 
199 
Instead, they are typically subjected to multiple stresses that may occur simultaneously or 
200 
sequentially over time [Jiang et al., 2025]. Consequently, plant stress responses are highly 
201 
complex and rely on the integration of diverse signaling networks involving phytohormones, 
202 
ROS, calcium, NO and H2S. The coordinated interplay among these signals enables plants to 
203 
fine-tune their physiological and molecular responses according to the nature, intensity, and 
204 
duration of the stress, as well as the developmental stage of the plant and the specific organ 
205 
affected. Figure 2 depicts a working model illustrating the signaling cascade triggered by 
206 
environmental cues, in which NO and H2S act as key signaling molecules coordinating plant 
207 
stress responses. 
208 
Future research should not only seek out new target proteins but also how these are 
209 
regulated in a cellular context in response to abiotic stresses. Thioredoxin (TRX)-mediated 
210 
denitrosation and depersulfidation are important processes that deserve further investigation. 
211 
Identifying the specific TRXs involved at subcellular compartments is essential, as these 
212 
small redox proteins constitute a diverse family that regulates distinct target proteins, often 
213 
through NAD(P)H-dependent mechanisms. Additionally, the application of advanced 
214 
techniques such as in situ imaging proteomics via expansion (iPEX), which combines 
215 
isotropic tissue expansion with matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) mass 
216 
spectrometry imaging [Wang et al., 2026], holds great promise for advancing our 
217 
understanding of NO- and H2S-derived PTMs. This approach could provide valuable insights 
218 
into their spatial distribution and dynamics under both physiological and, particularly, stress
